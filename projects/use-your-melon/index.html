Use Your Melon

Gesture-controlled installation challenging Georgia Aquarium guests to a game of echolocation charades.

summary

The Georgia Aquarium’s Dolphin Coast opens onto a pre-show atrium where guests wait — often the better part of an hour — in front of blank walls. For our Georgia Tech master’s thesis, we asked what that room could teach people about the animals they were about to meet.

Use Your Melon turns the wait into a game of charades. Guests strike a pose to mimic a sea creature, and a digital dolphin echolocates to work out what they are, then explains how the two of them meet in the wild.

my role

I led the guest research — five days of observation in the atrium, 14 interviews with guests, floor staff, and managers, and the affinity mapping that turned all of it into a design brief.

From there I designed the gesture interaction and built the demo. A Microsoft Kinect feeds depth data to a pose model, and Unity renders a responsive undersea scene around whoever is standing in front of it.

process

guest research

Five days of observation and 14 interviews with guests, floor staff, and managers, mapped down to four opportunities.

participatory design

A workshop with five aquarium stakeholders turned those opportunities into concepts we could prototype on paper.

how to play

step right up

A dolphin companion greets you beside a bubblized version of yourself.

strike a pose

Find the hidden gesture for one of six creatures and the dolphin starts echolocating to identify you.

transform

Hold the pose, burst into bubbles, and learn how that creature lives alongside dolphins in the wild.

outcomes

  • — Completed as a Georgia Tech MS-HCI master's thesis advised by Dr. Carrie Bruce
  • — Designed in partnership with the Georgia Aquarium for the Dolphin Coast pre-show atrium
  • — Shipped a feature-complete Unity and Kinect demo recognizing six creature poses
  • — Captioned in English and Spanish, the two most common languages spoken on the aquarium floor
  • — Evaluated with four aquarium stakeholders and five physical-interaction experts after COVID-19 closed the floor to testing
